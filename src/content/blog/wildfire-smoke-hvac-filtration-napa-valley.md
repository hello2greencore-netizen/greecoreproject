**Short answer:** for wildfire smoke in Napa Valley, the HVAC setup that helps most is a system that can keep you comfortable with every window closed, a filter slot built for a better filter, and ducts sealed so they don't pull in attic or crawlspace air. Smoke often shows up on hot days, so reliable cooling is part of the plan, not a separate one. Fall and winter, after fire season, are the best time to get it ready.

Napa Valley homeowners have lived through enough smoky Septembers to know the routine. This post is about the setup side: what to change in your home's system before next season, so the smoky days are easier to ride out.

## Why smoke season is harder in Napa Valley

The valley is long and narrow, with hills on both sides. When smoke drifts in, it can sit in the valley for days instead of blowing through.

Inland heat adds to it. Up-valley towns like St. Helena and Calistoga run hotter than the city of Napa in summer, and smoke events often line up with heat. If your only way to cool off is opening the windows at night, smoke season leaves you choosing between hot and smoky.

The housing mix matters too. Napa has plenty of older homes, from bungalows near downtown to ranch houses from the middle of the last century. Many have ducts in a crawlspace or attic, older filter grilles, and gaps that let outside air in.

## A filter setup that can handle smoke

The filter does most of the work during a smoke event, so it's worth getting this part right.

- **Start with the filter slot.** Many older systems have a thin filter behind a return grille. That slot only fits thin filters, and some leak air around the edges. A dedicated filter cabinet at the furnace or air handler can hold a thicker media filter that catches more fine particles without choking the airflow.
- **Pick a filter your system can breathe through.** Higher MERV ratings catch smaller particles, but a dense filter in a thin slot can restrict airflow. We check what your blower and return ducts can handle before recommending one.
- **Seal the filter so air can't sneak around it.** A good filter does little if air bypasses it through gaps in the rack or a loose grille.
- **Keep spares on hand.** Filters load up fast in heavy smoke. Have at least one replacement in the garage before fire season starts.

## Staying cool with the windows closed

This is where a lot of Napa Valley homes fall short. A house without [air conditioning](/services/air-conditioning), or with an old AC that struggles on hot afternoons, pushes people to open windows at exactly the wrong time.

A few options, depending on the home:

| Your home now | Option to consider |
|---|---|
| Ducts and an older furnace, no AC | A ducted [heat pump](/services/heat-pumps) that heats and cools through the existing ducts |
| Old AC that can't keep up | Replacement sized to the home, plus a duct check |
| No ducts (older cottage or add-on room) | Ductless mini split heads in the main rooms |

Whatever the system, we size it to the house: insulation, windows, sun exposure, and duct condition. That way it can hold a comfortable temperature on a hot, smoky day without anyone cracking a window.

## Sealing ducts and leaks before next season

If your ducts run through a crawlspace or attic, leaks let the system pull in unfiltered air from those spaces and push it into your rooms. During a smoke event, that air can be smoky.

Common problems we find in older Napa homes:

1. **Disconnected or crushed duct runs** in the crawlspace.
2. **Unsealed boots** where the duct meets the floor or ceiling register.
3. **Leaky return ducts**, which pull air from wherever the leak is, straight into the system.
4. **Old flex duct** that has torn or sagged over the years.

[Duct work](/services/duct-work) repairs and sealing help in smoke season, and they help with comfort and energy use the rest of the year too. A quick look in the crawlspace or attic usually tells us whether the ducts are part of the problem.

Outside the system, simple weatherstripping on doors and windows helps keep smoke out. A portable HEPA air cleaner in the bedroom adds another layer for the nights you need it most.

## A pre-season checklist for Napa homeowners

Late fall through spring is the easy time to do this work. HVAC schedules are calmer, and you're not making decisions during an emergency.

- Have the system checked so it's ready for the first hot spell.
- Ask whether your filter slot can be upgraded to hold a better filter.
- Get the ducts inspected for leaks and disconnects.
- Make sure any fresh air or ventilation setting can be closed off during smoke.
- Stock filters for both your central system and any ductless heads or portable cleaners.

## Common questions

### Should I upgrade my filter or my whole system for smoke season?

Often the filter setup and the ducts are the first fix, and they cost less than a new system. If your home has no reliable cooling, adding it can matter just as much, because it lets you keep the windows closed.

### Can a heat pump help during wildfire smoke?

Yes. A standard heat pump recirculates indoor air through your filter and cools the house without opening a window. It does not pull in outside air on its own.

### Do ductless mini splits filter smoke?

Each indoor unit has a small washable filter that catches some particles, but it isn't designed as a high-efficiency smoke filter. Pairing mini splits with a portable HEPA air cleaner works well in bedrooms.

### When is the best time to prepare my HVAC for fire season?

Fall, winter, or early spring. You get easier scheduling and the work is done before the first smoky, hot week arrives.
